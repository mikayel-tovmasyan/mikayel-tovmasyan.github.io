Research

My research lies in public economics and political economy, with a particular focus on offshore finance and the economic consequences of geopolitical policies and political transitions. I examine how financial sanctions and electoral turnovers affect offshore banking, alongside broader questions in public economics about educational policy. My work combines large-scale banking and administrative data, leaked offshore records, and citizen-generated observations with empirical approaches to causal inference, including regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, event studies, and synthetic control.

Job-market paper

Bypassing Sanctions:
Hide 'N Seek in Tax Havens

with Dominika Langenmayr and Sebastian Vosseler

Revise and resubmit, Journal of International Economics

Do financial sanctions induce targets to move assets beyond the sanctioning country's reach? We combine quarterly BIS data on bilateral bank deposits with U.S. and EU financial sanctions from 1996–2023 and exploit variation in sanction timing across countries. Individual sanctions increase deposits in tax havens: by about 18% after U.S. sanctions and 46% after EU sanctions. Deposits simultaneously fall in sanctioning jurisdictions, especially after EU sanctions. When sanctions are more severe because the U.S. and EU sanction jointly, haven deposits do not increase on average; deposits in other foreign countries fall and domestic deposits rise. Major sanctions likewise restrict foreign deposits more broadly. Combining leaked offshore data with individual-level sanctions, we find that sanctioned individuals rely on existing shell companies, while other residents establish new offshore structures, potentially anticipating further sanctions.

Work in progress

Elections and Offshore Deposits: Evidence from Close Electoral Turnovers

with Sebastian Vosseler

Do electoral turnovers discipline offshore finance by raising accountability and scrutiny? Using bilateral quarterly BIS data on cross-border bank deposits, we study whether national power transitions triggered by narrowly decided electoral turnovers affect offshore holdings. The effect is theoretically ambiguous: increased enforcement expectations and the loss of political protection may reduce offshore deposits, while political uncertainty, capital flight, or rent extraction may increase them. Using a regression discontinuity design and a difference-in-differences analysis, we find that offshore deposits decline by 5–10 percent following close presidential electoral turnovers, consistent with an accountability channel in which new governments raise enforcement expectations and prompt politically exposed individuals to adjust their offshore exposure. Preliminary evidence suggests that part of this response reflects a shift toward less transparent offshore structures rather than full repatriation. These findings show that political transitions can reshape private portfolio decisions in opaque jurisdictions, even without new transparency reforms.

The Education Gambit: Chess, Cognitive Skills, and a Natural Experiment in Armenia

This paper examines whether compulsory chess instruction improves cognitive skills and long-term participation and success in competitive chess, exploiting Armenia’s mandatory three-year chess instruction in the primary-school curriculum since 2011. Using data from the Kangaroo mathematics and Russian Bear language contests (2009–2019) and the TIMSS international math and science assessments, and employing various difference-in-differences designs, the paper finds no statistically significant effects on the contest outcomes while finding a 0.27-standard-deviation increase in mathematics grades in the TIMSS data, mediated through skills in applying mathematical knowledge. In line with the unofficial goal of Armenia’s mandatory chess policy, and using rich national and international competitive chess data coupled with difference-in-differences and synthetic control designs, the paper additionally finds strong positive effects at lower levels of competitive chess in Armenia. However, this effect dissipates among higher-ranked groups in both national competitions and international FIDE competitions. Taken together, the results do not establish that compulsory chess caused statistically and economically significant gains in either cognitive performance or competitive chess. These mixed findings suggest that policymakers should weigh the potential benefits of compulsory chess instruction against its costs and alternative uses of classroom time before adopting or expanding such programs.
